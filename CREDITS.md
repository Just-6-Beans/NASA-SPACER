## Data and NASA material

Two NASA datasets drive the rover pass, and NASA's own descriptions back the three launch systems, the rover's rockets and the rock samples. Snapshots are committed in `data/nasa/`, each recording its source URL, provider and retrieval date (14 or 15 November 2026). The game reads the snapshots and never calls an API.

- **Earth to Mars light time: JPL Horizons** (NASA/JPL Solar System Dynamics), https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/horizons.api. Mars observed from Earth's centre, daily, 2026 to 2030. The game uses the one-way light time on the player's operations date as the signal delay. Horizons gives geometry only; the game makes no launch-window claim from it.
- **Night temperature: Curiosity REMS weather feed** (NASA/JPL-Caltech and Centro de Astrobiología, CSIC-INTA), https://mars.nasa.gov/rss/api/?feed=weather&category=msl&feedtype=json. This is an outreach product, not calibrated science data, and it covers one place, Gale Crater. The game uses the minimum air temperature of one sol per operations date and shows the sol number and date beside it.
- **Launch systems: NASA pages and reports.** `data/nasa/launch-vehicles.json` keeps, for each fact, the exact sentence it rests on. Sources: *The Space Shuttle* (NASA reference page), https://www.nasa.gov/reference/the-space-shuttle/; *40 Years Ago: Columbia Takes Flight!* (NASA History); *What Was the Space Shuttle? (Grades 5-8)* (NASA STEM); the STS-88 and STS-135 mission pages; *International Space Station Facts and Figures*; the Kennedy Space Center Shuttle and station FAQ (archived); the *Commercial Crew Program Press Kit*, https://www.nasa.gov/commercial-crew-program-press-kit/; NASA's 2020 certification article and Crew-2 release for Crew Dragon; two NASA Commercial Crew blog posts and an image article on the Atlas V; NASA's releases on the Starliner Crew Flight Test and its uncrewed return; and three publications of the NASA Office of Inspector General (report IG-20-005 of 2019, and news items of June and September 2026). Two disagreements between NASA sources are recorded with their quotes, one of them from the fact sheet *Space Shuttle Era Facts* (2011). Every URL is in the snapshot.
  - The game takes two numbers from these: thrust over weight at lift-off (Space Shuttle 6.8 million lb over 4.5 million lb; Falcon 9 "more than 1.7 million" lb over 1,207,920 lb) and the launch price (1 cr for each $10 million of NASA's cost figure).
  - These are descriptions and headline figures, not a performance dataset, and the cost figures are not like for like. NASA gives no lift-off weight for the Atlas V with Starliner, so its ratio is our estimate and the game says so.
- **The rover's rocket, the stuck-rover rescue and the sample selection: NASA and JPL pages.** `data/nasa/rover-tasks.json` (built by `scripts/fetch-rover-tasks.mjs`, retrieved 15 November 2026) keeps, for each fact, the exact sentence it rests on; the script downloads every source and stops if a sentence is no longer there. Every URL is in the snapshot.
  - Rockets: the Mars Science Laboratory and Mars 2020 launch press kits (JPL), JPL's release on Curiosity's launch, NASA's *Mars 2020 Liftoff* image article, NASA release 16-089 on the Mars 2020 launch contract ($243 million), NASA's launch-contract releases for IMAP ($109.4 million) and DART ($69 million) on Falcon 9, and *What Was the Saturn V? (Grades 5-8)* (NASA STEM).
  - Rock samples: NASA Science's *Mars Rock Samples* catalog, https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-2020-perseverance/mars-rock-samples/, for samples 1, 2, 11, 12 and 25; NASA and JPL releases of August and September 2021, 15 September 2022 and 10 September 2025; and *Meet the Mars Samples: Sapphire Canyon*.
  - Pictures: the five photos in `public/nasa/samples/` are the ones NASA's catalog prints beside each sample (credit NASA/JPL-Caltech), downloaded by the same script; each is checked against its caption on the page. They show the drill hole and its surroundings, not the core tube.
  - Real events: *NASA's Curiosity Rover Frees Its Drill From a Rock* (JPL, PIA26723, May 2026), *An Algorithm Helps Protect Mars Curiosity's Wheels* (JPL, 2017) and *NASA's Opportunity Rover Rolls Free on Mars* (JPL, 2005).
  - The game takes these numbers from them: the rocket price (1 cr for each $10 million: Atlas V 541 24 cr, Falcon 9 11 cr) and the spacecraft's launch mass (Curiosity 3,893 kg from NASA; Mars 2020 about 4,019 kg, which is our sum of that figure and the 126 kg by which NASA says Perseverance is heavier).
  - **Not NASA, and labelled so in the game:** the 4,020 kg a Falcon 9 can send to Mars is SpaceX's published figure (https://www.spacex.com/vehicles/falcon-9/; the snapshot script cannot read that page, so it is marked "by hand"). That Mars Sample Return was left unfunded in January 2026 is from a news report (The Register, 16 January 2026). That the Falcon 9 of 2011 was too small for Curiosity is the team's own research. The Falcon 9 price is NASA's cost for a different mission and stands in for a launch NASA has never bought.
  - The Atacama rock stuck to Curiosity's drill, not to a wheel. The game's card says so, and its telemetry is not rover data.
- **The station tasks, the dust storm and the photographs of Earth: NASA and JPL pages.** `data/nasa/station-tasks.json` (built by `scripts/fetch-station-tasks.mjs`, retrieved 15 November 2026) keeps, for each card, the exact sentences it rests on; the script downloaded each page and found all 30 quotes. Sources: Apollo 13: Mission Details (NASA); NASA Achieves Water Recovery Milestone on International Space Station (NASA, 20 June 2023); 25 Years Ago: Fire Aboard Space Station Mir (NASA Johnson Space Center, 23 February 2022); Station Trio Practices Emergency Before Radiation, Physics Research (NASA space station blog, 15 January 2019); Counteracting Bone and Muscle Loss in Microgravity (NASA, 1 December 2023); NASA's Record-Setting Opportunity Rover Mission on Mars Comes to End (NASA release, 13 February 2019); Martian Dust Storm Grows Global; Curiosity Captures Photos of Thickening Haze (NASA/JPL-Caltech, 20 June 2018). The eight photographs of Earth shown in the Cupola were taken by International Space Station crews and come from the NASA Image and Video Library (images.nasa.gov), saved in `public/nasa/earth/` with NASA's own captions: iss071e570863, iss073e0422279, iss072e001649, iss069e003192, iss071e449837, iss061e123823, iss069e005526, iss068e021122. Credit: NASA. The ideas for the tasks come from the team's mission spec (`TasksList.pdf`).
- **Station greenhouse: NASA plant research, as context.** The six crops follow NASA's reports of crops grown in space (lettuce, radish, mizuna mustard, kale, chile peppers, dwarf tomatoes). Two cards from the team's mission spec are quoted as research context: the Advanced Plant Habitat (a closed chamber with more than 180 sensors) and the chile peppers grown for 137 days in orbit, with a Dutch study of crops in Mars soil simulant. No NASA dataset drives the greenhouse: every good zone, timer, calorie and water figure, the pump failure and the score is our model, and the game says so.
